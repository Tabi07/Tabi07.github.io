Six days after Alphabet posted the first negative free cash flow quarter in its history, Meta reported earnings of its own — and Wall Street's reaction landed with almost identical force. Shares fell as much as 10.4% on the day, and the number underneath that drop was even starker than Google's: Meta's free cash flow didn't just shrink, it collapsed 91% year-over-year, from $8.55 billion down to $784 million. Two of the four biggest AI spenders on earth, six days apart, both showing the same symptom. That pattern is exactly what's making this feel less like a one-company story and more like the first dominoes falling in sequence.

## The Numbers That Spooked Investors

On the surface, Meta's quarter looked genuinely strong. Revenue came in at $60.8 billion, a 28% year-over-year increase near the top of management's own guidance. The core advertising business is healthy by any normal measure — ad impressions up 14%, average price per ad up 12%. This wasn't a company whose product stopped working.

What tanked the stock was everything beneath the revenue line. Earnings per share came in at $6.18, missing analyst estimates of $7.22 — a 13% year-over-year decline in earnings even as revenue grew nearly a third. Free cash flow, the actual cash left over after the business pays its bills and funds its investments, cratered to $784 million, down from $12.4 billion just the previous quarter and $8.5 billion a year earlier. And the capital expenditure driving that collapse hit $31 billion in a single quarter — enough that, as one analyst pointed out, Apple doesn't spend that much on capex across an entire year.

Meta also narrowed its full-year 2026 capex guidance to $130–145 billion, raising the low end of that range from $125 billion. For a company whose entire business model has historically been advertising, not infrastructure, that's an extraordinary sum — and it's before accounting for the fact that Meta, unlike Alphabet or Amazon, doesn't have a large existing cloud business generating outside revenue to offset the spend.

## Why This Hit Different Than Previous Meta Sell-Offs

This isn't actually the first time Meta stock has dropped double digits over AI spending — it happened in October 2025 too, when the company warned capex would be "notably larger" in 2026. But investors had a full two quarters to sit with that warning and, by most accounts, had made peace with it. Big Tech capex guidance had become almost background noise by mid-2026; "Magnificent Seven" stocks were hitting record highs even as spending forecasts climbed.

What changed this time is that the spending stopped being a forecast and became an actual line on the income statement. A senior analyst at Forrester put the shift plainly: Meta's AI spend was easy to celebrate when margins were still expanding — it's a much harder sell once the costs are visibly eating into the numbers investors actually see reported. Guidance is a promise. A 91% free cash flow collapse is a receipt.

## The Alphabet Echo Is the Real Story

Here's what makes this moment feel different from any single company's earnings miss: Meta's cash flow wipeout landed within days of Alphabet reporting its own first-ever negative free cash flow quarter, and multiple outlets covering Meta's drop explicitly framed it as an echo of what had just happened at Google. That's not a coincidence of timing — it's the same underlying dynamic showing up at two different companies with two different business models, within the same earnings season.

Zoom out, and the pattern gets bigger than just these two. Collectively, Big Tech hyperscalers are projected to spend somewhere between $650 billion and $725 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026, with AI-related debt issuance across the sector projected to reach roughly $570 billion. That's an entire industry simultaneously financing an infrastructure buildout large enough to require historic levels of borrowing, at the same moment cash flow is visibly buckling under the weight of it at more than one company.

## What Zuckerberg Is Actually Betting On

To be fair to Meta's side of this, the spending isn't undirected panic — there's a specific strategic bet behind it, and Zuckerberg laid it out clearly on the earnings call. The plan involves personal AI agents working continuously on behalf of users, business-facing agents handling customer service at scale, and — notably — a new ambition to sell AI compute directly to other companies, essentially moving Meta toward the cloud-infrastructure business model that's cushioning Alphabet and Amazon's own spending sprees. Zuckerberg even mentioned fielding "a number of offers at a meaningful premium" over what Meta paid for some of its compute, hinting at real external demand for capacity Meta has already built.

That's a genuinely different story than "we're spending because everyone else is." If Meta can actually stand up a compute-reselling business, that gives its AI infrastructure a second revenue stream beyond internally improving Facebook and Instagram — something the company has never really needed before, but increasingly resembles what Google and Amazon have leaned on to make their own numbers work.

## The Honest Complication: This Has Happened Before, and Meta Recovered

It's worth resisting the urge to treat this drop as proof the sky is falling, because Meta's own recent history complicates that reading. This is at least the third time in under a year that a Meta earnings report triggered a double-digit single-day stock decline over AI capex concerns — it happened in November 2025, again around Q1 2026 guidance, and now again in Q2. Each time, the stock initially plunged on spending fears and then, at least so far, found its footing again as the underlying ad business kept delivering strong growth. Whether this time is different — a genuine inflection point rather than the fourth iteration of the same investor jitters — is precisely the question nobody can answer yet from a single earnings report.

## My Take

I don't think the "first domino" framing is quite right, but I don't think it's wrong either — it just needs a correction. Meta isn't the first domino. Alphabet, six days earlier, was. What Meta's report actually confirms is that this isn't an Alphabet-specific accounting quirk or a one-time Q2 anomaly — it's a structural feature of what it now costs to compete in AI infrastructure at the top of the industry, showing up in the financials of the two companies best positioned to absorb it.

That's actually the more unsettling read, not the less unsettling one. Alphabet has an 82%-growing cloud business and a $514 billion contracted backlog cushioning its bet. Meta doesn't have an equivalent cloud business yet — it's explicitly trying to build one, live, in the middle of this spending cycle, which is a meaningfully riskier position than Alphabet's. If the company with the weaker hand structurally is posting a 91% free cash flow collapse, the companies further down the AI spending list, with smaller balance sheets and less diversified revenue, have a much harder version of this same problem coming, whether or not this specific quarter turns out to be the peak.

The honest answer to "is this the first domino" is that we won't know until we see whether Meta's Q3 looks like a rebound (the pattern its last two capex-driven sell-offs eventually followed) or a continuation. A single negative quarter, even a historic one, could plausibly be a timing artifact of an unusually large capex delivery window. A second consecutive negative quarter would be much harder to wave off as a blip, and would be the clearest signal yet that this isn't a temporary investment spike — it's the new baseline cost of staying competitive in AI, for every company chasing the same buildout with a fraction of Alphabet's balance sheet.
